# Sight Lines

## Overview

Sight lines are the deliberate control of player visibility in a level. By designing what the player can see — and what they can't — level designers guide exploration, build tension, control pacing, and direct player movement without explicit instructions.

## Design principles

### The destination rule
If the player can see a goal, they'll move toward it. Place interesting landmarks, lights, or structures at key positions to pull players in the intended direction.

### Controlled revelation
Don't show everything at once. Use geometry, lighting, and fog to reveal areas gradually. The player should always have something to discover around the next corner.

### Vertical sight lines
Looking up or down creates curiosity. A structure visible above suggests there's a way up. A distant floor below suggests a path forward.

### Blocked sight = anticipation
When you can't see what's ahead, tension builds. Corridors that curve, doors that obscure rooms, and elevation changes that hide what's beyond all create anticipation.

## Techniques

### Framing
Use doorways, arches, and environmental elements to frame a view. This tells the player "look here" without text.

### Leading lines
Architectural elements (railings, paths, light beams) that point toward objectives or points of interest.

### Beaconing
Placing a distinctive visual element (colored light, unique structure, moving object) visible from multiple locations to serve as a navigation anchor.

### Theweenie (Disney term)
A tall, visible landmark that can be seen from everywhere in the level, serving as a constant orientation point.

### Opacity layers
Multiple layers of visible depth — foreground objects partially obscuring background ones — create a sense of scale and invite exploration.
